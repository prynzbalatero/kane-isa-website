Know Your Rights May 6, 2026

Citizens Arrest, Castle Doctrine & General Self Defense

People have no clue what they're legally allowed to do when violence happens right in front of them. They freeze, guess, or trust bad information from social media and TV. This post breaks down California law in plain terms.

Know where your rights begin, where they end, and what "reasonable force" can actually mean when seconds matter. This isn't legal advice — it's the information you should already have.

Citizens Arrest

California Penal Code § 837

A private person may arrest another:

  • For a public offense committed or attempted in his presence.
  • When the person arrested has committed a felony, although not in his presence.
  • When a felony has been in fact committed, and he has reasonable cause for believing the person arrested to have committed it.
Part 2. Of Criminal Procedure — Chapter 5. Arrest, by Whom and How Made [833–851.93] — Enacted 1872
Real World Example

Someone is beating up your grandma. You can "arrest" that person using reasonable means of force.

Key Takeaway

Reasonable use of force is subjective to your county and state. What's considered "reasonable" will be evaluated after the fact — so understanding the standard before you act is critical.

Castle Doctrine: Home Defense

California's Castle Doctrine gives you legal standing to defend yourself and your household within your own home. This is one of the most misunderstood areas of self-defense law.

Key Takeaway

"Reasonable" use of force can be applied to defending yourself, your domicile, and your family from any unauthorized party accessing your property or threatening persons inside.

For a detailed breakdown of California's castle law, see: esilverlaw.com — Castle Law in California

General Self Defense

California Penal Code § 197 — Justifiable Homicide

Homicide is also justifiable when committed by any person in any of the following cases:

  • When resisting any attempt to murder any person, or to commit a felony, or to do some great bodily injury upon any person.
  • When committed in defense of habitation, property, or person, against one who manifestly intends or endeavors, by violence or surprise, to commit a felony, or against one who manifestly intends and endeavors, in a violent, riotous, or tumultuous manner, to enter the habitation of another for the purpose of offering violence to any person therein.
  • When committed in the lawful defense of such person, or of a spouse, parent, child, master, mistress, or servant of such person, when there is reasonable ground to apprehend a design to commit a felony or to do some great bodily injury, and imminent danger of such design being accomplished.
  • When necessarily committed in attempting, by lawful ways and means, to apprehend any person for any felony committed, or in lawfully suppressing any riot, or in lawfully keeping and preserving the peace.
Amended by Stats. 2016, Ch. 50, Sec. 67 (SB 1005). Effective January 1, 2017. — Source: California Code, Penal Code - PEN § 197. Current as of January 01, 2025.
Example 1

If in fear for your life or the lives of others within your home, you may be able to detain or use lethal force against an intruder displaying severe intent to harm, maim, or kill any residents of the domicile.

Example 2

You are able to use force against someone who is currently committing a violent felony in your presence.

Conclusion

The law gives people options to defend themselves, their family, and others in serious situations — but those options come with consequences if misused. Force is judged after the fact, not in the moment, so knowing the line matters.

Read the code, understand the risk, and don't confuse confidence with legality.
